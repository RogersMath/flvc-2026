The last mile can be 4,000 miles away.

Jesse Rogers & Mike Ba

A tutor in Florida and a teacher in Senegal built one rope across the gap. This page follows our talk from top to bottom. Everything on it is yours to reuse.

Act 1The call

A phone call from four thousand miles away

One day my co-presenter, Mike Ba, called me with his class on speakerphone so I could hear their applause, their cheers and their thanks. It was for something I had built for them with AI, in a language they speak and I don’t.

Mike is the reason it reached them. He knows their languages and their needs, and he put it in their hands.

I hope every educator gets an experience like that sometime in their life.

Social media, AI and video calls are why it is possible. Mike and I met in a Facebook forum in 2022. Four years later, the last mile can be crossed, even when it is 4,000 miles long.

Mike taught himself English, and he speaks five languages. He is the only English speaker in his village. He could have pursued opportunity in the West, and who would blame him? He decided to stay and help the children of Senegal.

To strengthen the bonds we have started, we need your help.

— Jesse

Act 2One person at a time

I sit with one student at a time

As a tutor I am only ever face to face with one student. For a long time the question was how to explain an idea to the person in front of me.

Now a build costs a few sentences to an AI partner. Building got cheap. Knowing what to build did not. So I ask one question: what would change this one person’s life? Then I build that, for that person, and I teach them to build too.

I don’t think in terms of repositories or course adoption. From where I sit, that isn’t the problem I have. It is a problem others solve at a scale I can’t reach. My pages are public on the open web, which is also how AI tools can theoretically find them.

— Jesse

Act 3In conversation with Mike

Mike teaches in a village in Senegal

Mike speaks five languages and taught himself English. He is the only English speaker in his village. In the room, he answers these questions himself, and what he shares about his students is his to choose.

  1. Who are your students, and what is your village like?
  2. What changed for you when you connected to the wider world?
  3. What do your students most need?

If there is time: what he is growing

Moringa is nébéday in Wolof and gawdi in Pulaar, and it already grows wild in Senegal. The research report Mike and I wrote together argues that the gap in remote villages is less about scarcity than about knowledge: what to plant, how to prepare it, and how to protect it.

Mike has been working to get moringa adopted and nurtured. The results so far are mixed: animals eat it. That is a rough map, and we would like help correcting it.

Awareness runs both ways. Researching moringa for Mike put it in my kitchen. I sometimes stir it into my porridge.

Act 4One number

How many are still waiting for the call

~3 in 4 children living in extreme poverty live in sub-Saharan Africa.

The region is home to about 23% of the world’s children, but to more than 312 million of the children in extreme poverty. Across the past decade, the World Bank and UNICEF found no improvement there.

Source: World Bank and UNICEF, Child Poverty: Global, Regional and Select National Trends (September 2025), as reported by AllAfrica. Totals differ by poverty line and release.

Act 5Fast and durable

Individuals are fast. Institutions are durable.

Two groups built this road: the technologists who spent decades making it possible to connect any two people on Earth, and the open education community that filled it with something worth carrying.

I can build something overnight for one student. By myself I can’t make it last, make it trustworthy, or carry it to the next thousand students, but together we as an educational community can do that.

— Jesse

Act 6How you can help

We need people who have done this before

Mike and I built the first rope. We have never written a grant or run anything bigger than a learning center or a classroom, so we need people who have.

  • Most wanted

    You have built programs that last

    If you have taken a working model and turned it into a program that lasts, that is who we most want to talk to.

  • Welcome

    You speak French

    Review French materials, make something with Mike’s students, or write back to them.

  • Both ways

    You have partnerships of your own

    If there are relationships you would like to learn from us about, we are glad to share what we have learned.

Thirty minutes is all we’re asking for.

ExtraQuestions we expect

Honest answers

Isn’t AI-built content full of errors?

Grounding in lookup and citations has improved a great deal, and on well-documented subjects like calculus I trust frontier models more than I trust myself. Where the data is thin, as with Wolof and Pulaar, a local speaker has to confirm and adjust. Verification has moved. It hasn’t vanished. Mike can tell you how well AI translates across his five languages.

Can one person’s builds last?

Not on their own. Individuals prototype, and institutions sustain. That is why we are asking for your help.

What about students without AI access?

This works for Mike because he has a connection and a computer. Extending that is part of the ask.

How do you feel about AI training on open work?

Jesse’s view: ideas are like air, free to breathe in and out. Many instructors feel differently, especially about their own voice or image. A dedication on a specific gift is a separate matter, which is why the Garden is licensed no-derivatives.

Why not put everything in a repository?

Jesse’s answer: that is my seat, not a verdict on yours. Repositories do vetting, trust and scale. I work one person at a time.
